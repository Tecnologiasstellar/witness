On 1 January 2021, China stopped productive fishing along the main stream and
major tributaries of the Yangtze River for ten years — a whole river basin
closed for a decade, with no obvious precedent. Five years in, the two animals
most often named when the ban is discussed have moved in opposite directions.
The Yangtze finless porpoise, which had declined for as long as anyone had
counted it, rose from 1,249 animals in 2022 to 1,426 in 2025. The Chinese
sturgeon, which spawns below a dam completed in 1981, has not turned around at
all. The difference is not effort. It is which pressure a fishing ban reaches.

## What the river had already lost

The baiji, the Yangtze river dolphin, was last seen in 2002 and declared
functionally extinct in 2007, on the strength of a six-week survey by teams
from seven countries. Wang Ding, of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, has said
his research group was set up for the baiji back in 1978, when about 300 were
thought to remain; reserves were gazetted and money was spent, and the number
went down anyway. What a river loses when that happens is
[its own hard question](/p/what-happens-when-a-species-goes-extinct): there was
no baseline to measure the absence against.

The porpoise was on the same path. Roughly 2,700 in the early 1990s, about
1,800 by 2006, around 1,040 by a 2012 government survey, 1,012 in 2017. The IUCN moved
it to [Critically Endangered](/p/what-does-critically-endangered-mean) in 2013. What separates it from the
baiji is mostly that the decision to act at river scale arrived while there
were still enough animals for it to matter.

## What a ten-year closure costs

The ban was not a signature on a document. By 2023, 111,000 fishing boats had
been withdrawn and 231,000 fishers had left the trade. By the third quarter of
2025, 142,000 of them who wanted work had been placed in other industries and
220,000 were enrolled in pension insurance. Fifteen provinces and
provincial-level cities are involved, coordinated by the Ministry of
Agriculture and Rural Affairs.

It had been proposed long before. The academician Cao Wenxuan and sixteen
colleagues put a ten-year ban forward in 2008 and were, in National Science
Review's account, questioned and then ignored for years.

## Why one animal and not the other

Ping Zhuang, of the East China Sea Fisheries Research Institute, put the
asymmetry plainly: the porpoise was endangered mainly by a food shortage
overfishing had caused, so it is the direct beneficiary of a fishing ban. The
sturgeon is endangered mainly by habitat destruction — its spawning grounds
lost to hydropower — which a fishing ban cannot touch.

The sturgeon's own record is worse than the summary suggests. NOAA Fisheries
puts the adult population at 32,260 before 1981, 6,000 by 2010 and 2,569 by
2015, and records that the species failed to breed naturally in 2013, 2015,
2017, 2018 and possibly 2019. Hatchery-reared juveniles keep going into the
river in their hundreds of thousands each year, which keeps the species
present without making it self-sustaining.

## What the protected area is doing

Alongside the ban there is a network of places. Writing in October 2025, the
Chinese Academy of Sciences counted eight in-situ reserves on the main stream
and in Dongting and Poyang lakes, covering more than 30 percent of the river's
middle and lower reaches, and four ex-situ reserves in quiet oxbow lakes
holding over 150 porpoises between them, with roughly ten calves born a year.
One of them, the Tian'ezhou oxbow in Hubei, is a national nature reserve for
the baiji. It was set aside for an
animal that did not make it, and it is breeding the one that did.

That is the argument for protecting whole systems rather than single species,
and also its limit. What share of the planet gets protection of any kind is
[a separate count](/p/how-much-of-the-earth-is-protected). A closed river gave the porpoise its food back. It did not
give the sturgeon its spawning ground back, and nobody involved claims it will.
Qiwei Wei, who has worked on the sturgeon for decades, says the recovery will
be long and will need the spawning grounds rebuilt; Zhuang says it will require
giving up some of what the dams provide.

National Science Review's interviews with the researchers running the
monitoring — Chen, Wei, Zhuang, Gui — are the clearest account of what the
first five years measured, and they are free to read. Our
[record for the Yangtze finless porpoise](https://witnessatlas.com/archive/yangtze-finless-porpoise)
holds the animal itself.
