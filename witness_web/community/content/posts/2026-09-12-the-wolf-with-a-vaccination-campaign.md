Between March and November 2019, rabies and canine distemper moved through the
Ethiopian wolves of the Bale Mountains at the same time. Writing in *Emerging
Infectious Diseases* in December 2024, Marino and colleagues recorded an event
that touched at least 19 packs across four subpopulations and lasted more than
220 days. Single-pathogen outbreaks in these wolves had run about three months.
This one did not behave like the emergencies the programme had been built to
answer, and the response to it is the reason the vaccination strategy looks
different now.

## What the survey teams found

Field teams recovered 57 carcasses and watched another five animals showing
advanced clinical signs. Of the animals tested, seven were positive for rabies
virus and 13 for canine distemper; one carried both. In the two areas under
close enough monitoring to measure a before and after, the Sanetti Plateau lost
about 60 per cent of its wolves, with 17 unaccounted for, and the Web Valley
about 53 per cent, with 47 unaccounted for.

Those are not projections. They are the difference between a known population
and the animals still there when the teams went back, which is the only kind of
mortality figure a small, individually-known population can produce, and the
reason so much of this work is monitoring rather than intervention.

## The vaccine had to be tried on wild wolves first

An oral rabies vaccine was not a given. A bait can be refused, a sachet can go
unpierced, and none of that is visible from a distance. So the University of
Oxford, the Ethiopian Wildlife Conservation Authority and the UK Animal and
Plant Health Agency ran a field trial of the SAG2 vaccine in three wolf packs
and published the result in *Vaccine* in 2016.

The arithmetic is worth reading at full precision. Of 21 wolves trapped after
the bait was laid, 14 carried the biomarker showing they had taken it. Of those
14, 86 per cent reached antibody levels considered sufficient to protect
wildlife, and half cleared the higher threshold recognised universally. Count
from the 21 rather than the 14 and a little over half of the wolves trapped
carried antibody levels in the protective range. All but one of the vaccinated animals were alive fourteen months later.

"We now have a safe vaccine, a suitable bait, an efficient delivery method, and
trained monitoring teams in place," said Claudio Sillero-Zubiri, who began the
long-term study of these wolves in 1987. His colleague Fekede Regassa put the
stakes in the same release: four major rabies outbreaks, he said, had each
crashed the Bale Mountains population, "typically killing 50-75% of the
subpopulation affected."

## Reacting, then preventing

For years vaccination was reactive — an outbreak was detected and the packs
around it were treated. Oral vaccination campaigns ran in 2018 and 2019, and the
2019 event arrived anyway, with a second pathogen alongside the one the vaccine
addressed. The authors of the *Emerging Infectious Diseases* paper drew the
obvious conclusion and stated it plainly: vaccines covering both rabies and
distemper "should be favored when possible, in light of the possibility of
concurrent outbreaks."

The campaign has since shifted toward vaccinating before anything is burning. In
2025, according to the Wildlife Conservation Network, which funds the work, more
than 40 wolves were vaccinated across 14 packs against both diseases. That is
close to a tenth of every Ethiopian wolf alive. It is a real number and a modest
one, and both halves of that sentence matter.

## Who is actually doing this

The Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme was founded in 1995 and runs as a
WildCRU endeavour in partnership with Dinkenesh Ethiopia, the Ethiopian Wildlife
Conservation Authority and regional governments, with Girma Eshete as field
director. Since 1996 it has also vaccinated the domestic dogs in the settlements
around wolf country — more than 3,000 a year — on the reasoning that a disease
with a reservoir is not solved inside the species you are trying to save.

The animal at the end of all this is rare enough to count individually. Fewer
than 500 adults survive across six isolated populations, and the Wildlife
Conservation Network gave the figure as 454 as of this writing. The species was
listed as Critically Endangered in 1994 after a crash, and down-listed to
Endangered in 2004 when the numbers came back.

The programme publishes its own monitoring and its own papers, and the 2024
outbreak paper is open to read. The
