Ploughshare tortoises in northwestern Madagascar carry serial numbers cut into
their shells. Staff at Baly Bay National Park put them there on purpose, and
the Durrell Wildlife Conservation Trust and the Turtle Conservancy both list the
engraving among the measures protecting the species. The reasoning is that a
collector pays for an unblemished shell, so a permanent mark should destroy the
price. It is the rare conservation intervention that looks, to anyone who does
not know why, exactly like vandalism. It did not stop the trade.

## The problem the mark was built for

Most of the species on any endangered list are there because something took
their habitat. The ploughshare is not one of those. Durrell names illegal
collection for the pet trade as the leading cause of its decline, and the shell
that draws buyers is the same shell the animal cannot put down. Where demand
rather than land use is doing the killing, protecting habitat does not touch the
mechanism, and a range state cannot reach the buyer.

Engraving was an attempt to reach the buyer anyway, from inside Madagascar,
without a treaty or a court. A marked tortoise is not a pristine specimen. The
theory was that a market organised around perfection would refuse it.

## What happened instead

By September 2016 the answer was already in. Andrew Terry of Durrell told
Mongabay that his team had watched marked animals being offered for sale on
Facebook earlier that year. Elizabeth Bennett, then vice president for species
conservation at the Wildlife Conservation Society, gave the two reasons the
marks failed: with effort, an engraving can be made less conspicuous, and in any
case "some buyers are clearly willing to buy marked animals."

That is worth sitting with rather than rushing past. The assumption inside the
tactic was that the buyer wanted a perfect object. What the market showed is
that some of them wanted the animal, scarred or not.

The marks did do something the people who made them did not advertise. A
numbered tortoise is an identifiable tortoise, which is why Durrell's staff
could recognise their own animals in a sale photograph at all. The engraving
failed as a deterrent and worked as evidence.

## The numbers underneath

Field surveys across Baly Bay between 2006 and 2015 recorded the wild population
more than halving, to an estimate of about 500 adults and subadults in 2014 and
2015, according to Durrell, which also reports that the great majority of the tortoise
seizures recorded between 2002 and 2016 came after 2009. The Turtle Conservancy states that the species was
considered functionally extinct in the wild by 2017 and puts the wild remnant at
around 100 animals. The Turtle Survival Alliance lists it, flatly, as presumed
functionally extinct in the wild.

Every one of those figures carries a date because the gap between them is the
story. Nothing about this animal is fast, and the decline was.

## Where the work went

Durrell has paused releases into the wild, saying plainly that the poaching
threat continues, and keeps breeding at its centre at Ampijoroa. The Turtle
Conservancy has taken the other route: a group of tortoises confiscated from
smugglers and brought to its sanctuary at Ojai, California, produced 25
hatchlings in 2025, the first of the species bred outside Madagascar, and the
organisation has launched a one-million-dollar rewilding initiative aimed at
eventually returning their descendants.

Neither of those is a rescue. They are what is left to do when the animal is
worth more to a collector than the law can make it cost, and both organisations
publish their own programme pages, which is where the reading starts. Our
[record for the species](https://witnessatlas.com/archive/ploughshare-tortoise)
links them.
