No. Extinct in the Wild is a specific category on the IUCN Red List, with a
written test attached, and the axolotl does not meet it. *Ambystoma mexicanum*
is listed as Critically Endangered, the category below it, and was most
recently assessed in 2019. The question is a reasonable one to ask, though. In a survey
of the canals of Xochimilco in southern Mexico City reported in August 2025,
the nets came up empty. What the researchers found instead was the animal's
genetic material, loose in the water.

## The category is narrower than the phrase

The Red List's categories are defined in a document anyone can download, and
Extinct in the Wild has two parts. A taxon qualifies when it "is known only to
survive in cultivation, in captivity or as a naturalized population (or
populations) well outside the past range." It is *presumed* Extinct in the Wild
when "exhaustive surveys in known and/or expected habitat, at appropriate times
(diurnal, seasonal, annual), throughout its historic range have failed to record
an individual."

Both halves matter here. Axolotls are still recorded in Xochimilco, so the first
half fails. And a survey that detects an animal without catching one has
recorded it, so the second half fails too. The category is not a description of
how bad things are. It is a claim about evidence, and the evidence still points
the other way.

## What the counts counted

The numbers that get repeated about the axolotl are densities, not totals — how
many animals per square kilometre of canal, which is what you can actually
measure in a waterway you cannot drain. Writing in *BioScience* in 2015, Voss,
Woodcock and Zambrano put the decline like this: "The density of axolotls
decreased from 6000 per square kilometer in 1998 to 100 in 2008," and added that
"recent data suggests that there are fewer than 35 axolotls per square
kilometer."

The last full count was made in 2014, and Conservation International, which
supports the monitoring, puts that year's result at 36 per square kilometre. The
two published figures differ by roughly one animal per square kilometre, which is
worth noticing only because it shows how little is left to round. The 2019 Red
List assessment put the surviving wild population somewhere between 50 and
1,000. A range that wide is not evasion. It is what an honest estimate looks like
when the thing being estimated has become hard to find.

## The survey that caught nothing

The most recent effort, run by the Ecological Restoration Laboratory of the
Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México with support from Conservation
International-Mexico, worked 115 monitoring sites across the 2,500 hectares of
the Xochimilco protected area. For the first time it used environmental DNA
alongside nets: water is collected and searched in a laboratory for the cells
and traces an animal sheds as it passes.

No axolotl was netted. The eDNA found them anyway, in the less disturbed water
and in several of the chinampa refuges — restored versions of the raised
farming plots that have worked this wetland since before the city grew over it.
"Unlike previous surveys, this time we included environmental DNA to better
understand where axolotls are surviving and how their habitat is changing," Luis
Zambrano, who directs the laboratory, told Conservation International. Twenty-one
of those chinampa refuges are now running, with biofilters to clean the water.

## The other axolotl

The reason the question gets asked at all is that axolotls are simultaneously
common. Nearly every one in a laboratory tank or a pet shop descends from a
small shipment out of this same lake system: 34 animals that reached Paris in
1863, of which, the *BioScience* authors write, "From just five males and one
female, the Jardin des Plantes distributed thousands of axolotls to scientists
throughout Europe." The domesticated line was founded on six animals and has
been bred continuously ever since. It has no bearing on the wild population,
which is why the Red List counts them separately.

## What is actually being tried

In April 2025, Ramos, Mena, Schneider and Zambrano published the first real test of putting captive-bred axolotls back. They
released small groups — eight animals into a constructed pond in a university
reserve in 2017, five pairs into a restored chinampa in 2018 — and tracked them
by radio tag. The animals survived the tracking period and foraged. Two that
were recaptured had put on weight, one of them by more than 18 grams. Movement
peaked at water temperatures of around 16 to 17 degrees, which the authors read
as a narrow thermal preference. After the study ended, a great egret took one of them,
and local farmers reported a second.

That is the state of the evidence: an animal that is hard to find, not one that
is gone, and a small number of people testing whether the water can be made to
hold it again. The release paper is open access, and the
[record we keep for the species](https://witnessatlas.com/archive/axolotl) links
the reading behind it. If the category itself is the confusing part,
[what critically endangered actually means](/p/what-does-critically-endangered-mean)
is the longer answer.
