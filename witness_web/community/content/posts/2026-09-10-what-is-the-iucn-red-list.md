The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species is a published assessment of how likely
a species is to go extinct, produced by the International Union for
Conservation of Nature's Species Survival Commission together with the IUCN
Global Species Programme and a partnership of member organisations. It sorts
species into categories against a written standard and records the evidence
behind each one. It is not legislation. A species placed in a threatened
category gains no legal protection from being placed there. Protection comes
from national laws and international treaties, decided by other bodies, under
different criteria, on their own timetable.

## Who does the assessing

The route to publication is formalised, and the formality is the point. The
IUCN Red List Unit is the gatekeeper: every assessment that appears on the list
has to be submitted to it and pass its final checks.

The assessments themselves come from Red List Authorities, which are not
individual people but standing groups appointed under the Species Survival
Commission — usually its taxon-specific Specialist Groups, except where a
partner institution holds the role instead. BirdLife International is the Red
List Authority for all birds.

Within that process the roles are separated on purpose:

- **Contributors** gather and supply the raw data.
- **Assessors** apply the categories and criteria to a species and document how
  they reached the result.
- **Reviewers** check it. At least one named reviewer must be independent of
  the assessment, must not be the same person as the assessor, and must sign a
  declaration of conflicting interests.

Assessments also arrive from IUCN's own global assessment programmes and from
external projects run by universities, individuals and national red list
initiatives; those have to be signed off by the relevant Red List Authority's
coordinator. Two standing bodies may also check any assessment before
publication for consistent application of the criteria across taxonomic
groups.

A published listing can also be challenged. A petition against a current
listing goes to a process the Standards and Petitions Sub-Committee arbitrates,
and the Red List Authority is required to abide by what it decides.

## What the standard says about its own reach

The rules live in *IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria*. The category system
was adopted by IUCN Council in 1994; the current version, 3.1, was approved in
2000, published in 2001 and reissued in a second edition in 2012.

Its introduction is careful about a distinction that rarely survives into
secondary explainers. The system exists to classify species by extinction risk,
and while it may focus attention on the taxa at highest risk, the document says
plainly that it is not the sole means of setting priorities for the
conservation measures that would protect them.

A later section makes the point harder. A category of threat is not necessarily
sufficient to determine priorities for conservation action, because the
category assesses extinction risk under current circumstances, whereas deciding
what to do involves cost, logistics, the chance of success and the biology of
the particular case.

That is the whole distinction. A category is a reading of risk. A priority is a
decision about money and effort. The standard does not confuse the two.

## The law is a separate machine

In the United States, whether a species is legally endangered is decided under
the Endangered Species Act on five statutory factors: destruction or
modification of habitat, overutilisation, disease or predation, the inadequacy
of existing regulatory mechanisms, and other natural or manmade factors
affecting its continued existence. The determination must be made solely on the
best scientific and commercial data available, and any interested person or
organisation may petition to add a species, remove it, or reclassify it.

Read the fourth factor again. It asks whether existing regulation is working —
a question about statutes, not about animals. Nothing in the IUCN criteria asks
it; they measure rate of decline, size of range, number of mature individuals
and modelled probability of extinction. The two systems can be applied to the
same species and return answers that do not line up, because they were built to
answer different questions.

## A global label, read locally

The categories were designed for global assessments of a whole species. The
standard warns that a global category may not match a national or regional one:
a species of Least Concern worldwide can be at severe risk in one country at
the margin of its range, and a globally threatened species can be secure in a
place where it happens to be doing well. Anyone quoting a category is quoting a
scale as well as a label.

## Reading an entry honestly

The update published on 9 July 2026 brought the list to 175,909 assessed
species, 49,505 of them threatened. Those are counts of assessments, not a
census of life.

An entry tells you which group assessed a species, under which version of the
standard, on what evidence, and when. It does not tell you that anyone is
obliged to do anything about it. The US Fish and Wildlife Service records that
the ʻalalā, the Hawaiian crow, has been extinct in the wild since 2002 and
survives in captivity — and Extinct in the Wild is precisely the category the
Red List keeps for that situation. The category is the record of the fact.
Whatever happens to [the bird itself](/archive/hawaiian-crow) will be done by
the people running the aviaries and the releases.

It is the same caution that applies one category down, where the label is
[routinely quoted in place of a number](/p/what-does-critically-endangered-mean).
