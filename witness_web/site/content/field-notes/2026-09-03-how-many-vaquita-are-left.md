The most recent published survey of the vaquita, carried out in the northern
Gulf of California between May and September 2025, recorded between seven and
ten individuals, with roughly two-in-three confidence in that range, and at
least one and possibly two calves. That figure counts animals the observers
could see and identify. It is not a full population estimate: the last complete
abundance estimate was made in 2015, and a new one drawn from the 2025 acoustic
data had not been published as of this writing.

## The number is a count of sightings, not a census

Two ships worked the northern Gulf through September 2025, each carrying three
pairs of 25x binoculars and a rota of experienced observers. Where they looked
was decided by acoustic detectors moored through the summer, spaced between one
and five kilometres apart, which register the clicks a vaquita makes to navigate
and feed. Photographs and drone footage from every sighting were compared
against a catalogue of known individuals, some of them identifiable by the shape
of a dorsal fin.

The result is then produced by expert elicitation: the people who did the
watching are asked, formally, how many distinct animals they believe they saw,
and how confident they are. That method sounds soft next to a statistical
abundance estimate, and it is used because the honest alternative does not
exist. At these numbers, a conventional line-transect survey has almost nothing
to work with. A range of seven to ten, held with about two-in-three confidence,
is a more truthful object than a single number carrying a decimal point.

## Why the pages you find disagree

NOAA's vaquita abundance page still closes on the figure it was written around:
approximately 30 animals, as of November 2016. NOAA's own species directory,
updated in March 2026, says fewer than 20 remain and that the species may now
number as few as ten, and notes that the last *full* survey took place in 2015.
Conservation organisations publish round numbers of their own.

None of those pages is dishonest. They are dated differently, they answer
slightly different questions, and a search engine flattens all of them onto one
screen. When a number matters, take the one whose source says when it was
measured and what it measured — and treat any figure without those two things as
a rumour with a citation attached.

## What changed in 2025

The surveys of the three preceding years, run by the same teams and methods, give
the only useful comparison. In 2023 the elicited range was eight to twelve. In
2024 it was six to eight, and the observations inside the protected area came in
lower than expected — which is why the 2025 effort was widened to cover water the
earlier surveys had not searched. In 2025 the range was seven to ten, with at
least one calf, one of them belonging to a female known as Frida, recognisable by
a bent dorsal fin, who has now raised calves in three consecutive years.

The 2025 report is careful about what this does and does not show. There was no
decline of the magnitude recorded in the worst years, when the population fell by
nearly half in a single year in both 2017 and 2018. Vaquitas are still
reproducing, and some calves are surviving their first years. Most detections
came from inside the small zone where enforcement is concentrated, and some came
from water just outside it, which is the finding with a consequence attached.

## What the number cannot tell you

Nothing in the 2025 result changes the cause. Vaquitas drown in gillnets,
particularly those set illegally for totoaba, and the International Union for
Conservation of Nature has listed the species as Critically Endangered since
1996; it is listed as endangered under the United States Endangered Species Act.
A count that holds steady across two years is not a recovery. It is the absence
of another collapse, measured with wide uncertainty, in a population small enough
that one bad season would take back everything the last three added.

The next real datum is the abundance estimate still to come from the 2025
acoustic work. Until then, the honest answer to the question in the title is a
range, a date, and a method — which is also the whole
[record we keep for this species](/witnesses/vaquita).
