Critically Endangered is the highest of the three threatened categories on the
International Union for Conservation of Nature's Red List. A species earns it by
meeting at least one of five criteria indicating an extremely high risk of
extinction in the wild. Those criteria measure different things: how fast a
population is falling, how small its range is, how few breeding adults are left,
and the modelled probability that it disappears. Meeting any single one is
enough. The category is a statement about risk, not a count of survivors.

## Where the category sits

The Red List has nine categories, running from Not Evaluated to Extinct.
Critically Endangered, Endangered and Vulnerable are the three grouped together
as threatened, and they nest: anything listed Critically Endangered would also
qualify as Endangered and as Vulnerable. The category above it is Extinct in the
Wild, which is not a matter of risk at all but of where the last individuals are.

## The five criteria

The thresholds come from a document called *IUCN Red List Categories and
Criteria*, version 3.1, first published in 2001 and reissued in a second edition
in 2012 that corrected the presentation without altering the system itself. For
Critically Endangered, an assessor is looking for any one of these:

- **A. Decline.** A population reduction of at least 90% over ten years or three
  generations, whichever is longer, where the causes are understood, reversible
  and have stopped — or at least 80% where they have not.
- **B. Range.** An extent of occurrence below 100 square kilometres, or an area
  of occupancy below 10 square kilometres, together with at least two further
  signs: severe fragmentation or a single location, continuing decline, or
  extreme fluctuation.
- **C. Small and falling.** Fewer than 250 mature individuals, with a continuing
  decline alongside it.
- **D. Small.** Fewer than 50 mature individuals.
- **E. Modelled.** A quantitative analysis putting the probability of extinction
  in the wild at 50% or more within ten years or three generations.

Each species is evaluated against all five, and every criterion it meets at the
highest threat level is recorded. But only one has to be met.

## Why it is not a headcount

Three of those five doors are not about how many animals are alive. Criterion A
is a rate — a species that has lost four-fifths of its population in three
generations qualifies whether the survivors number in the hundreds or the tens of
thousands. Criterion B is about geography. Criterion E is a probability produced
by a model. Only C and D are thresholds on abundance, and even they count
something narrower than a population: the standard defines population size as the
number of mature individuals only, assessed in the wild and inside the natural
range. Animals in captivity are not in the total.

Which is why two species can share the category and look nothing alike. The
[Javan rhino](/archive/javan-rhino) and the
[Sumatran orangutan](/archive/sumatran-orangutan) are both listed Critically
Endangered. One is counted in tens, the other in thousands.

## What the standard admits about itself

The document is candid in a way its summaries rarely are. It observes that
extinction is a chance process, so a higher category means a higher expectation
of extinction over the stated period — not a sentence passed on a species.

It adds a point that almost never survives into secondary accounts: a species
persisting for years in a high-risk category does not mean the original
assessment was wrong. The listing was a probability, never a date.

And on the numbers themselves, the standard concedes that the thresholds were
arrived at through wide consultation and set where they were generally judged
appropriate, even though no formal justification exists for those particular
values. They are a considered convention, and the document says so in its own
preamble rather than leaving anyone to discover it.

## Reading a category honestly

A category tells you what an assessor concluded, under a named version of a
standard, at the time of the assessment. It does not tell you how many are left,
when they were last counted, or whether anything has changed since. Those are
three separate questions, and the category is routinely quoted in place of all
three. When the number is what you actually want,
[ask what the number counts and when it was measured](/field-notes/how-many-vaquita-are-left).
