## Premium dossier

### Field note

This Field Season opened in Mexico's sea, with a small porpoise almost
no one has seen. It closes in Mexico's water again — in the canals of
Xochimilco, on the southern edge of Mexico City, where the last wild
axolotls live among thousand-year-old floating gardens [S1].

You know this animal's face: the feathery gills like a headdress, the
permanent small smile. It is on Mexico's fifty-peso bill, in video
games, on murals. But the famous pink creature is a laboratory bloodline
— wild axolotls are dark, mottled like canal mud [S2][S9]. And the
Aztecs knew the truth of it before anyone: they named it for Xolotl,
the god who transformed himself into an axolotl to escape death. The
god who refused to die [S3]. This chapter is about whether that refusal
holds.

### The past — the lake that became a city

Five great lakes once filled the Valley of Mexico, and the axolotl
lived in them beside the island city of Tenochtitlan, where it was sold
in the markets — the Florentine Codex calls it food of the lords
[S3][S4]. After the conquest came four centuries of draining: flood
control, farmland, streets, until a metropolis of more than twenty
million people stood where the water had been. Of the five lakes, what
mostly remains is Xochimilco — a lattice of canals threading between
chinampas, the artificial garden-islands Aztec farmers built and their
successors still farm [S1][S5].

The survivors' refuge then turned hostile. In the 1970s and 80s,
tilapia and carp were introduced as food fish — and ate the axolotl's
eggs and young. The canals came to depend on treated wastewater;
pollution seeped from every direction. The census numbers fell like a
stone dropped in dark water: roughly 6,000 axolotls per square
kilometer in 1998, about 1,000 in 2003, about 100 in 2008, and by 2014,
around 36 [S1][S6]. Meanwhile — the great paradox — the axolotl became
one of the most abundant laboratory animals on Earth, descended largely
from a shipment of 34 sent to Paris in 1863: by some estimates a
million or more in tanks worldwide, famous for regrowing limbs, spinal
cord, heart tissue, even parts of the brain, with a genome ten times
the size of ours [S7][S8][S9].

A creature that can regenerate almost anything cannot regenerate a
lake.

### The present — nets, DNA, and eighteen brave animals

In late 2024, scientists from Mexico's national university, UNAM, began
the first full census in a decade, working 115 sites across the
protected wetland. The nets, cast again and again, caught **zero
axolotls**. But the water itself testified: environmental DNA — the
molecular traces a body sheds — confirmed axolotls still live in the
canals, unseen. Present, invisible; the full results are due in 2026
[S1][S6].

And in 2025 came the evidence that changes the ending. Researchers led
by Alejandra Ramos and Luis Zambrano released eighteen captive-bred
axolotls — ten into a restored chinampa refuge in Xochimilco, eight
into a spring-fed wetland across the city — each carrying a tiny radio
transmitter. **All eighteen survived the study.** Recaptured animals
had gained weight: they were hunting, in the wild, on their own. Two
were eventually taken by egrets — which is itself a kind of good news;
being prey is part of being wild. "The amazing news is that they all
survived," Ramos said. "They're hunting" [S10][S11].

The refuge they thrived in is the oldest idea in the valley: UNAM works
with chinampero farmers to seal off canal stretches with biofilters of
wood, gravel, and native plants — cleaning the water and walling out
carp and tilapia. Depending on the count and date, 21 to 36 such
refuges now exist, covering perhaps half of one percent of the canal
network. Where they exist, water clears, native fish return, and the
farmers' harvests improve [S1][S12].

**Known:** the axolotl persists in the wild, captive-bred animals can
survive and hunt there, and the refuge model works — for the animal and
the farmer both [S1][S10]. **Unknown:** the true wild number (the last
completed count found ~36 per square kilometer; official estimates span
50 to 1,000 mature animals); whether lab-lineage axolotls carry the
right instincts and genes for canal life; and whether Xochimilco's
water can outlast the thirst of a megacity [S6][S10][S13].

### The future — what would improve its odds

1. **Multiply the refuges.** From half a percent of the canals toward a
   connected sanctuary system — built with, and profitable for, the
   chinampa farmers whose ancestors built the lake's gardens in the
   first place [S1][S12].
2. **Fight for the water.** Biofilters and better treatment of the
   inflow are the axolotl's life support; every improvement also
   protects two hundred bird species and a city's flood buffer [S1][S14].
3. **Keep the invaders out.** Barriers and removal of carp and tilapia
   are what make a refuge a refuge [S12].
4. **Release, watch, learn.** The 2025 study proved survival is
   possible and taught the next lesson — train captive axolotls to fear
   herons before the water takes them back [S10].

Zambrano says it best: "If we can restore this habitat and restore the
axolotl's population in a city of more than 20 million people... we
have hope for humanity" [S11]. The god refused to die. Eighteen small
ambassadors just showed he might be right.

### Timeline of evidence

- **14th–16th century** — Axolotls thrive in the five lakes around
  Tenochtitlan; named for the god Xolotl; sold in the markets [S3][S4].
- **1521 onward** — The centuries-long draining of the Valley of
  Mexico begins [S5].
- **1863** — Thirty-four axolotls are shipped to Paris; six survivors
  found the laboratory lineage behind most captive axolotls today [S7].
- **1970s–80s** — Carp and tilapia introduced; the canals shift to
  treated wastewater [S5].
- **1987** — Xochimilco inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage with Mexico
  City's historic center [S14].
- **1998–2014** — The census crash: ~6,000 → ~36 per km² — 99% in
  sixteen years [S6].
- **2006** — Listed Critically Endangered [S13].
- **2018** — The 32-billion-letter axolotl genome is published — among
  the largest ever sequenced [S8].
- **2021** — The axolotl 50-peso note enters circulation; it is named
  banknote of the year, and millions of Mexicans keep it rather than
  spend it [S15].
- **2024–2026** — First census in a decade: nets find none; eDNA finds
  them. Full results due 2026 [S1][S6].
- **2025** — April: all eighteen released captive-bred axolotls survive
  and hunt in the wild [S10].

*Timeline last verified 2026-08-27.*

### A reflective prompt

The axolotl never grows up — and never gives up. Its refuge is not a
fence in the wilderness but a farmer's canal in a city of millions,
proof that the wild can live next door if the neighbors want it to.
What would it take for the place you live to keep one wild thing alive?
