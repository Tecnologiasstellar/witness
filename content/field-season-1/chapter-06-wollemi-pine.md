## Premium dossier

### Field note

This chapter's subject cannot run, hide, call, or care that you are
reading about it. It is a tree — the first plant in this Field Season —
and it may be the oldest story any living thing on Earth can tell.
About forty-five mature Wollemi pines grow wild today, in four small
stands at the bottom of one canyon system in Australia's Blue Mountains
[S1][S2]. Their pollen is indistinguishable from fossil pollen ninety
million years old. Their lineage watched the dinosaurs leave [S3].

And here is the one thing you must know before anything else: **nobody
will tell you where they are.** Not us, not the botanists, not the
government of New South Wales. The secret is not a tease. It is the
species' strongest shield — and this chapter is partly about why.

### The past — ninety million years, then a rope

The Wollemi pine's family flourished across the ancient southern
supercontinent. Then Australia dried out, epoch after epoch, and the
rainforest lineages retreated to the last damp, fire-sheltered places —
until, about two million years ago, the tree's pollen vanishes from the
fossil record entirely. Science reasonably filed it as extinct [S3][S4].

On the 10th of September, 1994, a national parks ranger named David
Noble rappelled into a remote canyon on his day off and walked into a
grove of trees he could not name. Botanists could — barely: it was a new
genus, alive out of deep time. The director of Sydney's botanic gardens
called it "the equivalent of finding a small dinosaur alive on Earth"
[S2][S5].

Fame nearly finished what the ice ages could not. Within a decade,
trespassers had found the site — and on their boots came *Phytophthora
cinnamomi*, a root-rot pathogen confirmed in the largest stand in 2005.
The only known infection of the wild grove was carried in, almost
certainly, by uninvited admirers [S6]. That is why the location is
secret, and why even loving this tree from a distance is a form of
protection.

### The present — the fire, the rescue, the insurance policy

In the Black Summer of 2019–20, the Gospers Mountain mega-fire burned
about ninety percent of Wollemi National Park, and it came for the
canyon. What followed became famous: a covert operation, kept quiet to
protect the site, in which air tankers laid retardant along the canyon
rim while specialist firefighters were winched from helicopters into
the gorge to run irrigation lines and pump water, day after day, under
a sky full of smoke. The groves survived. Of forty-nine large trees,
only four escaped scorching — but the canopy held, and the canopy is
what saved them [S7][S8].

The counts since then are almost absurdly intimate: the 2025 national
recovery plan records **45 mature trees and 46 wild seedlings** (a
parallel official page says 46 adults and 43 juveniles — with a tree
that sprouts multiple trunks from one root, even "an individual" is a
judgment call) [S1][S2]. Genetically, the news is stranger still:
studies across hundreds of markers found **no detectable diversity at
all**. The wild Wollemi pines are, in effect, one plant, copied [S4].

So its people built the broadest insurance policy in conservation:
since 2006 anyone has been able to legally buy a cultivated Wollemi
pine, and citizen scientists have now recorded them growing in 27
countries; more than 210 young trees went out in 2024–25 to botanic
gardens on three continents; and over 835 saplings now grow at new
secret sites inside the park [S2][S9][S10].

**Known:** the wild population is counted nearly tree by tree; the
threats — a pathogen, a fire, a footprint — are precisely identified;
and the species now also lives in thousands of gardens that can never
all burn at once [S1][S9]. **Unknown:** whether the burnt juveniles
recover and new seedlings reach adulthood; whether the translocated
groves — planted, burned within months, replanted — will ever be
self-sustaining; and whether a species with essentially one genome can
adapt to a changing climate at all [S2][S4][S10].

### The future — what would improve its odds

1. **More hidden groves.** The recovery plan's aim is more wild sites,
   in more canyons, chosen by soil and fire shelter — so no single
   catastrophe can be the last one [S1][S2].
2. **Clean boots, closed doors.** Strict access control and
   decontamination are the whole defense against Phytophthora; the
   pathogen has no cure in the wild, only prevention [S6].
3. **Buy the tree; never seek it.** Every legally cultivated Wollemi
   pine funds conservation and thickens the insurance population. The
   public's greatest gift to this species is enthusiastic distance
   [S9].
4. **Guard the recovery time.** The groves survived one fire age; what
   they need is enough quiet years between fires to grow back — the
   central climate-era question for their keepers [S7][S8].

A tree that stood among polar dinosaurs is now guarded like a state
secret, watered by helicopter, and planted in backyards from Sydney to
Scotland. Ninety million years on, its fate has become a collaboration.

### Timeline of evidence

- **~90 million years ago** — Pollen identical to the Wollemi pine's
  enters the fossil record; Gondwana's forests [S3].
- **~2 million years ago** — The pollen vanishes; the lineage is
  presumed extinct [S3][S4].
- **1994** — September 10: David Noble finds living trees in a Wollemi
  canyon [S2][S5].
- **2005** — Sotheby's auctions the first cultivated saplings
  (AU$1.59 million); retail sales begin 2006. The same year,
  Phytophthora is confirmed at the wild site, likely brought by
  trespassers [S6][S11].
- **2019** — 436 saplings planted at new secret sites — burned months
  later in the Black Summer, before establishing [S2][S10].
- **2020** — January: the covert helicopter-and-irrigation operation
  saves the wild groves as ~90% of the park burns [S7][S8].
- **2023** — The full genome confirms near-zero genetic diversity [S4].
- **2024** — 30th anniversary: translocated saplings surpass 835; a
  global meta-collection ships to botanic gardens worldwide [S2][S9].
- **2025** — March 27: the third National Recovery Plan takes effect —
  45 mature wild trees, 46 seedlings [S1].
