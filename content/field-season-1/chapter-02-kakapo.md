## Premium dossier

### Field note

Here is a fact that no other animal in this Field Season can offer you:
every kākāpō alive has a name. Sirocco. Solstice. Hoki. Māhutonga. Not
nicknames for a few favorites — all of them, each wearing a small
transmitter backpack, each known personally to the rangers of New
Zealand's recovery program [S1][S2]. When a species can be counted like a
classroom — 236 birds at the start of 2026 — you are looking at one of
two things: the end of something, or the beginning [S2].

The kākāpō is the world's heaviest parrot, moss-green, whiskered, smelling
faintly sweet, and completely flightless. It cannot fly, but it climbs
twenty-meter trees with its claws and comes down like a parachutist with
wings spread [S1][S4]. And in 2026, this impossible bird is having the
best year of its modern history.

### The past — how the night parrot fell

For millions of years, the kākāpō's enemies came from the sky by day. So
it became a creature of the night and the forest floor, and its one
defense was to freeze — perfect stillness, perfect camouflage [S4]. Then
people arrived, bringing animals that hunt in the dark, by smell: rats,
cats, and stoats. Every kākāpō defense became a liability. The freeze
that hid it from an eagle simply holds it still for a stoat, and its
lovely musty scent is a beacon [S4][S5].

The fall was long and nearly total. In 1894, the conservationist Richard
Henry moved hundreds of kākāpō to an island refuge — and watched stoats
swim across within six years and destroy them all, one of conservation's
first great heartbreaks [S5]. By the 1970s, decades of searching had
turned up a few dozen birds, every one of them male. The species seemed
finished. Then, in 1977, about two hundred kākāpō were discovered on
Rakiura, Stewart Island — with females [S5]. Feral cats were already
killing them, so between 1980 and 1997 every bird that could be found was
evacuated to small predator-free islands. In 1995, the count bottomed
out at **51 birds** — and the modern recovery program began [S2][S5].

One more name matters here: Richard Henry — not the man, the bird named
in his honor. The last kākāpō ever found from the Fiordland mainland, he
carried genes the island flock had lost. In 1998 he fathered three chicks
before dying around 2010, at an estimated eighty years old. His line
still swims in the species' blood [S6].

### The present — where the kākāpō stands

The count at the start of 2026 was 236, including 83 breeding-age
females [S2]. And then the rimu trees — whose mass fruiting, every two to
four years, is the only signal that makes kākāpō breed — delivered a
great year [S4]. The first chick of the season hatched on Valentine's
Day. By early April, more than 95 had hatched: **the biggest kākāpō
breeding season ever recorded**, beating 2019's record of 73 [S2][S9].
Chicks only join the official count when they reach 150 days, so the new
total is still being earned, night by night — and some chicks have
already been lost, which the program reports as plainly as the wins [S9].

The recovery machine behind this is remarkable: transmitters that flag
when a female starts nesting, feeding stations that open only to the
right bird and weigh her as she eats, and the genome of essentially every
living kākāpō sequenced, so pairings can be chosen to protect what
diversity remains [S1][S7]. In 2023, ten males even returned to the New
Zealand mainland for the first time in about forty years — and promptly
taught everyone something by climbing the sanctuary's pest fence;
several were brought back south, and the boom of a kākāpō was heard on
the mainland again anyway [S8].

**Known:** the count is exact, because every bird is named and tracked;
breeding follows rimu mast; the enemies are introduced predators, not
mystery [S2][S4][S5]. **Unknown:** how long a kākāpō lives (possibly
sixty to ninety years — no one has ever watched a whole life); why
infertility and failed eggs remain stubbornly common despite genomic
care; and the final size of this record season, still being counted
[S1][S9]. Disease is a standing risk: in 2019 a single fungal strain
reached a tenth of the species and killed nine birds [S10].

### The future — what would improve its odds

1. **More safe ground.** The islands are near capacity. The great prize
   is Predator Free Rakiura — clearing 175,000 hectares of cats and rats
   would let kākāpō go home to the island where they were rediscovered.
   Nothing that large has ever been done [S3][S11].
2. **Keep the genetics working.** Every genome is sequenced; smart
   pairings and artificial insemination — once even delivered by drone —
   stretch the diversity of 51 survivors as far as it can go [S7].
3. **Hold the biosecurity line.** One swimming stoat undid Richard
   Henry's life work in 1894. Quarantine, checks, and vigilance are the
   unglamorous wall everything else stands behind [S5].
4. **Let them be wild where possible.** The program's 2026 strategy
   deliberately intervenes less — fewer handouts, more natural nests —
   aiming for birds that thrive rather than merely survive [S2].

The kākāpō was declared all but gone three separate times, and each time
someone went looking anyway. This year the night forests are fuller of
booming than they have been in living memory. Names keep being added to
the list.

### Timeline of evidence

- **1840s** — The species is described by European science; Māori have
  known and named it far longer [S1].
- **1894** — Richard Henry moves hundreds of kākāpō to Resolution Island;
  stoats swim over within six years [S5].
- **1977** — About 200 kākāpō found on Rakiura / Stewart Island; the
  first females confirmed by 1980 [S5].
- **1980–1997** — Every findable bird evacuated to predator-free islands
  as cats kill the Rakiura flock [S5].
- **1995** — The low point: 51 birds. The Kākāpō Recovery Programme is
  founded with the iwi Ngāi Tahu [S2][S5].
- **2010** — Richard Henry (the bird) dies at an estimated 80; Sirocco is
  appointed New Zealand's official Spokesbird for Conservation [S6].
- **2019** — Record 73 chicks fledge; the same year an aspergillosis
  outbreak kills nine birds [S9][S10].
- **2022** — Population reaches 252, the highest in about fifty years [S2].
- **2023** — Ten males return to the mainland at Sanctuary Mountain
  Maungatautari, the first mainland kākāpō in ~40 years [S8].
- **2026** — 236 birds at season start; the biggest breeding season on
  record — first chick on Valentine's Day, 95+ hatched by April [S2][S9].

*Timeline last verified 2026-08-26. Final 2026 fledgling totals publish
after chicks reach 150 days [S9].*

### A reflective prompt

Every kākāpō has a name, and it changed how people fight for them — it
is harder to lose "Solstice" than to lose "one of 236." What in your own
world becomes harder to neglect the moment you name it?
