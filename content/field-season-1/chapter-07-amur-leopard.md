## Premium dossier

### Field note

Forget the leopard you know — the one sprawled in an African acacia.
Now give it a winter coat seven centimeters deep, long legs for wading
through snow, and a tail thick as a scarf that it wraps around itself
at night. Put it in a forest where winters are long and brutally cold,
on the border where Russia meets China. That is the Amur leopard: the
rarest big cat on Earth — and, quietly, one of conservation's greatest
comebacks [S1][S2].

In 2007 there were perhaps thirty of them. Today there are about 130 in
Russia and 115 counted in China's border park, with at least 27 cats
walking between the two countries — well over two hundred, though no
one can give one exact total for an animal that ignores borders
[S2][S3][S4]. What makes this story remarkable is what it did *not*
take: no captive releases, no genetic rescue. The leopards did it
themselves, the moment people stopped killing them and their prey.

### The past — how a leopard ends up in the snow, alone

The Amur leopard's crisis was ordinary, which is what made it so
effective. Its coat — the thickest and most beautiful of any leopard —
had a price: poached pelts were offered for a few hundred to a thousand
dollars apiece in the 1990s [S5]. Its food — sika deer, roe deer, wild
boar — was poached too, for meat, so even an uncaught leopard could
starve. And every spring, farmers burned fields; between 1996 and 2003,
almost half of the leopard's remaining habitat burned at least once,
converting forest to open ground the cats avoid [S6].

Squeezed by fur, hunger, and fire, a cat that once ranged across
northeast China and Korea shrank to a single pocket of Russian forest
smaller than many counties. The censuses read like a countdown: around
forty in the 1970s, thirty-some in 1990, and by 2007 an estimate of
just **19 to 26 leopards left on Earth** [S1][S5].

### The present — what protection, by itself, can do

In April 2012, Russia gathered most of the survivors' habitat into one
protected area: Land of the Leopard National Park. Rangers went after
poachers and set fires; the deer came back; and someone finally counted
properly — the park now runs about three hundred camera traps, the
largest such network in Russia, and because every leopard's rosette
pattern is unique as a fingerprint, the cats can be known one by one
[S2][S7]. There is Typhoon, whose territorial call became the first
ever recorded from a wild Amur leopard; there is Kazanova, who walked
out of the park on his own and re-founded territory his species had
lost for fifty years [S8].

The numbers answered. The 2015 census announced the population had
doubled. By 2018 it passed one hundred. The most rigorous scientific
estimate — 118 leopards, published in 2025 — comes with a confidence
interval tighter than almost any wild cat population on Earth, and the
core-area density has nearly tripled since 2015 [S3][S9]. Across the
border, China built its own vast park; its count rose from 42 in 2017
to **115 in 2025**, with fifteen cubs in a single year [S4]. Even the
road got fixed: in 2016 Russia buried a highway inside the 575-meter
Narvinsky tunnel — the country's first — so leopards could cross their
mountain pass above the traffic [S10].

**Known:** the recovery is real, measured, and was achieved by
protection alone — anti-poaching, fire control, prey recovery, one
tunnel [S2][S3]. **Unknown:** the exact total (border-crossing cats
defeat simple addition); the cost of inbreeding in a population rebuilt
from a few dozen (kinked tails and white paws already whisper of it);
and the reach of canine distemper, a dog-borne disease confirmed in one
wild leopard, whose worst-case models are grim [S4][S11][S12].

### The future — what would improve its odds

1. **Grow into China.** Five cross-border corridors now connect the
   Russian pocket to a park system with room for many more cats — the
   species' way out of its own small map [S4].
2. **Found a second population.** In 2023, three leopards were released
   into Ussuriysky Reserve — the first deliberate step toward a backup
   population, with a breeding center planned in the species' old
   southern range [S8].
3. **Head off the disease.** Vaccinating even a few wild leopards and
   managing village dogs, models show, dramatically improves the odds
   against distemper [S12].
4. **Keep the insurance current.** More than two hundred Amur leopards
   in the world's zoos preserve 91% of the founders' genetic diversity
   — a reserve the wild population may someday need [S13].

The park's scientist Taisiia Marchenkova states the ambition plainly:
"We hope to see Amur leopards back across their historic range in two
decades" [S14]. Twenty years ago that sentence would have been a
eulogy. Now it is a schedule.

### Timeline of evidence

- **1857** — The subspecies is described from a leopard skin from
  Korea [S5].
- **1970s** — The range collapses to one pocket of southwest Primorye;
  gone from Korea [S5].
- **2007** — The low point: an estimated 19–26 individuals — the
  world's rarest big cat [S1][S5].
- **2012** — April: Russia creates Land of the Leopard National Park,
  covering most remaining habitat [S2].
- **2015** — Census: the population has roughly doubled since the low
  [S1].
- **2016** — March: the Narvinsky wildlife tunnel opens — Russia's
  first [S10].
- **2018** — The count passes one hundred; China formalizes its
  Northeast Tiger and Leopard National Park in 2021 [S4][S9].
- **2023** — May: three leopards released in Ussuriysky Reserve — the
  second-population project begins [S8].
- **2025–2026** — Peer-reviewed estimate of 118 in Russia; record
  density; China announces 115 with fifteen cubs in a year [S3][S4][S9].

### A reflective prompt

The Amur leopard needed no rescue technology — only for the killing,
the burning, and the taking to stop. How often is the most powerful
thing we can do for something simply to stop doing the harmful thing?
