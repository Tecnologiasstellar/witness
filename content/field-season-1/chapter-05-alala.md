## Premium dossier

### Field note

There is a category of endangered animal sadder than "critically
endangered." It is called **extinct in the wild** — a species that still
exists, but only behind fences. Since 2002, the ʻalalā, Hawaiʻi's crow,
has lived there: roughly 110 to 120 birds in two conservation centers,
and none at all in the forests that shaped them [S1][S2].

This chapter is about the day that stopped being the whole story. In
November 2024, five young ʻalalā stepped through an open aviary door on
the slopes of Haleakalā, on Maui — and as of January 2026, more than a
year later, **all five are still out there, alive and free** [S3][S4].
One pair has already built a nest and laid an egg. The people who raised
them wept at the door. So might you.

### The past — how the forest lost its voice

The ʻalalā is a crow the way Hawaiʻi is an island: the same idea,
transformed into something found nowhere else. Larger than mainland
crows, with bristly feathers about the bill, it mates for life and
speaks in dozens of calls — sweet chirps, rhythmic whoops, cries that
visitors describe as eerily human [S4][S5]. In Hawaiian tradition the
ʻalalā is ʻaumakua — an ancestral guardian — and it appears in the
Kumulipo, the creation chant. As one of its keepers, Keanini Aarona,
puts it: "These birds, they are our ancestors" [S4][S6].

The forest needed the bird as much as the culture did: ʻalalā swallow
and spread the seeds of more than thirty native plants — for some, they
were the only courier [S1][S7]. Then the threats arrived in layers:
forests cleared for cattle and cane; rats, cats, and mongoose finding
the nests; mosquitoes carrying avian malaria upslope; and the ʻio, the
native Hawaiian hawk, picking off young birds that no longer had elders
to warn them [S5][S7]. Seventy-six birds in 1976. Thirteen by 1992. In
2002, the last wild pair was seen in South Kona, and the wild story
ended [S1].

Two rescue attempts failed painfully: 27 birds released in the 1990s —
21 died. About 30 more between 2016 and 2020 — roughly 25 died, many
taken by ʻio, and the five survivors were brought back inside [S1][S8].

### The present — five birds on a new island

The program learned from every loss. And then it changed the map: Maui
has no ʻio at all. In November 2024, five juveniles — hatched in
conservation care, schooled to fear predators using a taxidermy hawk
with flapping wings, named by children from five Maui schools — were
released into the Kīpahulu Forest Reserve on Haleakalā's leeward slopes,
carrying the first GPS backpacks ever worn by their species [S3][S9].

Everything before this said they would die. Instead: after five months,
all five were foraging on native fruits, riding out storms, expanding
their vocabulary [S9]. After a year — all five, still. In late 2025 two
of them paired, built a nest, and produced an egg; the female sat the
full three weeks of incubation. First eggs rarely hatch, and this one's
fate is unknown — but a species that was extinct in the wild is nesting
in it again [S4]. Two more birds have been staged for release to join
them [S10].

**Known:** the five have outlived every previous release cohort's
trajectory; the trained fear of hawks held in the real forest; and the
whole species can use stick tools — a 2016 *Nature* cover study found
78% of tested birds do it spontaneously, making the ʻalalā one of only
two tool-using crows on Earth [S9][S11]. **Unknown:** whether the pairs
will raise chicks; and the hard ceiling underneath everything — every
living ʻalalā descends from just nine founders, and more than half of
the program's eggs fail to hatch, a burden written in the genes that no
single good year can lift [S12][S13].

### The future — what would improve its odds

1. **Grow the Maui flock.** Each new release into hawk-free Kīpahulu
   tests and teaches; the birds' own culture — warning calls, foraging
   knowledge — rebuilds only in the wild [S3][S10].
2. **Take back the mosquitoes.** A coalition is dropping roughly half a
   million lab-reared male mosquitoes per week over East Maui's forests
   — males that father no offspring — to crash the population carrying
   avian malaria [S14].
3. **Manage nine founders' worth of genes.** Genome-guided pairing is
   the program's quiet daily labor against the hatch-failure ceiling
   [S12][S13].
4. **Go home eventually.** Maui is a stepping stone; the long plan
   returns the ʻalalā to Hawaiʻi Island itself, once the hawk problem
   has an answer [S1][S3].

"In the deep past, people picked out that voice from all the voices of
the forest," says Martin Frye of the recovery project. "We can't lose
that voice" [S4]. For the first time in a generation, on one mountain,
no one has to.

### Timeline of evidence

- **1890s** — ʻAlalā still widespread on Hawaiʻi Island; the long
  decline begins [S1].
- **1976** — Seventy-six birds remain, in four forest pockets [S1].
- **Late 1970s** — The conservation flock is founded; ultimately nine
  genetic founders [S12].
- **1993–1998** — Twenty-seven birds released in South Kona; twenty-one
  die; the rest are brought back [S8].
- **2002** — The last wild pair is seen. Extinct in the wild [S1].
- **2016** — *Nature* cover: the species is a natural tool user [S11].
- **2016–2020** — About thirty released on Hawaiʻi Island; roughly
  twenty-five die; five survivors recalled [S1][S8].
- **November 2024** — Five ʻalalā released in Kīpahulu Forest Reserve,
  Maui — the first release beyond Hawaiʻi Island [S3].
- **Late 2025** — First wild nest and egg in a generation; two more
  birds staged for release [S4][S10].
- **January 2026** — All five confirmed alive past the one-year mark [S4].

### A reflective prompt

The ʻalalā's keepers spent twenty years failing in public and trying
again — and the fifth attempt is flying. What have you stopped
attempting after two failures?
