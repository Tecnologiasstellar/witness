### A pause, between chapters

You have now met a parrot whose island refuges were reached by swimming stoats, a crow held out of its own forest partly by a mosquito, and a tree nearly poisoned by what rode in on a bootsole. Ahead is a salamander whose canals filled with fish that were never supposed to be there.

This is the season's second pause, and its second pattern. The first pattern was a trade — deliberate, priced, criminal. This one is stranger, because most of it was never anyone's plan. It is the story of everything we bring with us.

### The pattern

Wherever people have gone, other species have traveled in our wake — in ship hulls and cargo holds, in ballast water and potted plants, on boot soles, and often on purpose, released with good intentions that curdled. The tilapia and carp in the axolotl's canals were introduced as food fish [S6]. The stoats in New Zealand were brought to control the rabbits that earlier settlers had also brought [S7].

The uninvited mean no harm. A stoat in a kākāpō burrow is simply being a stoat; the mosquito carrying avian malaria upslope is only following the warmth. That is what makes this pattern hard to hate and harder to stop: the fault line runs not through the creatures that moved, but through the moving itself.

The bill is now measured. The world's biodiversity panel assessed it in 2023: invasive alien species have played a major role in sixty percent of the world's recorded plant and animal extinctions where the causes are known — and were the sole driver in sixteen percent. The measured economic cost passed 423 billion dollars a year in 2019, and it has at least quadrupled every decade since 1970 [S1].

Even the season's quietest chapter belongs to this pattern. The organism found in the Wollemi pine's secret grove in 2005 — *Phytophthora cinnamomi*, a water mould, not a true fungus — was almost certainly carried in by admirers. The infection is documented and the threat is real and ongoing; the grove is not dying of it, and the quarantine exists so that sentence stays true [S5].

### The limits

Here is the hard part, told straight. Against established invaders, full eradication has only ever reliably worked in one arena: islands, and island-like places with defensible edges. No one has ever eradicated an established mammal invader from an entire continent. New Zealand's famous Predator Free 2050 goal — a whole nation without rats, possums, or stoats — openly depends on scientific breakthroughs that do not yet exist [S3]. And in 2025, the government wound down the dedicated company that funded its landscape projects, folding the work back into the conservation department — ambition and budget do not always travel together [S4].

There is also an honest ethical argument inside this fight. Eradication means killing, at scale, animals that did nothing but exist where we put them. Some scientists and advocates call that unjustifiable; others answer that a permanent eradication ends more suffering than control that never stops. Witness reports the debate; it will not pretend the question is easy [S8].

### What works — where the tide has turned

1. **Defend the defensible edge.** On Wellington's Miramar Peninsula, rats, stoats, and weasels were declared eliminated in 2023 — and wild kiwi now walk hills above the capital for the first time in living memory. Islands and peninsulas are where this fight is being won, one edge at a time [S2].

2. **Send a mosquito against the mosquito.** Over the forests of East Maui, and since February 2025 over Kauaʻi's Alakaʻi Plateau, helicopters and drones release lab-reared male mosquitoes whose matings produce eggs that never hatch. Tens of millions have flown; the aim is to crash the population carrying avian malaria — the same disease system that stands between the ʻalalā and its old forests [S9].

3. **Clean boots, closed doors.** The least glamorous lever is the strongest: quarantine. The Wollemi grove's secrecy and sterilized-boot protocols, and New Zealand's border biosecurity, are the pattern's true front line — keeping the uninvited from arriving at all [S5][S7].

4. **Build the wall in the water.** In Xochimilco, refuge canals are sealed with volcanic-rock filters and gates the carp and tilapia cannot pass — a wall that lets the water through and holds the invaders out, built with the farmers whose ancestors built the canals [S6].

### A reflective prompt

Every uninvited species had a courier, and the courier rarely knew. Look at your own boots, your garden, your luggage: what might you be moving right now, without meaning anything by it?

### Take action

- **Island Conservation** — Funds the removal of invasive species from islands — the one arena where eradication reliably works — and the return of the native wildlife that follows. — https://www.islandconservation.org/
- **Predator Free NZ Trust** — Funds and connects the backyard and community trapping groups working, edge by edge, toward a predator-free New Zealand. — https://predatorfreenz.org/
