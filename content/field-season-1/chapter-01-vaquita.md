## Premium dossier

### Field note

*Vaquita* means "little cow" in Spanish — an affectionate name for an
animal that has never asked for attention. If you stood on the shore of
the Upper Gulf of California at dawn, where the desert runs straight into
a warm green sea, you would probably miss it: a small grey back, a
ring-eyed face, one soft breath, gone. The smallest porpoise on Earth is
also the shyest, and its entire world is that one corner of sea — the
smallest range of any marine mammal [S1]. Most people who have fished
those waters all their lives have never seen one.

This is the story of how a gentle animal nobody hunts became the rarest
marine mammal alive — and why the people who know it best have not given
up on it. By the end, you may not want to either.

### The past — how the little cow got here

The vaquita's trouble began far from its sea, with a different animal
entirely. The totoaba is a big silver fish that shares the same water, and
its swim bladder — the balloon-like organ that keeps a fish afloat — is
treasured in parts of Chinese tradition as a delicacy and a status gift.
Dried, one bladder can fetch thousands of dollars; smugglers call it
"aquatic cocaine" [S2].

Mexico banned totoaba fishing back in 1975, and world trade was outlawed
in 1977 [S2]. But the price survived the ban, so the fishery simply went
underground, run more and more by trafficking networks. Poachers catch
totoaba with gillnets: walls of mesh hanging invisible in the water,
sized to wedge a large body and hold it. And a gillnet cannot tell one
animal from another. A vaquita that swims into one — set for totoaba, or
for shrimp — cannot back out, and cannot rise to breathe [S1][S3].

So the count fell: from about 567 animals in the first full survey of
1997, downward decade after decade — not because anyone wanted vaquitas
gone, but because every link of the chain held: a belief, a price, a net,
one small sea [S4][S5]. The chain has a human hinge, too. The fishing
families of San Felipe and El Golfo de Santa Clara built their lives on
the same nets, and a ban without real alternatives pushes honest fishers
toward the illegal economy it was meant to stop [S3][S6].

A chain like that can sound unbreakable. It is not. Every link in it is a
human choice — and choices can be unmade.

### The present — where the vaquita stands

Here is the sentence scientists waited years to write: **the little cow
is holding on.** Three annual surveys in a row have found no substantial
further decline — 8–12 individuals seen in 2023, 6–8 in 2024, and **7–10
in 2025, with one or two newborn calves** [S5][S9]. One mother,
recognizable by her bent dorsal fin and named Frida after the painter,
has now been seen with calves in three separate years [S5][S9]. In the
Zero Tolerance Area — the small guarded polygon where the last vaquitas
gather — illegal netting has fallen sharply since the Navy sank hooked
concrete blocks there to snag nets [S6].

And in 2022, geneticists delivered the decade's best news. Reading the
full genomes of twenty vaquitas, they found that the species' long
history of natural rarity left it carrying unusually little harmful
variation — meaning it is **not doomed by inbreeding**. In their
simulations, if deaths in gillnets stop, the vaquita most likely
recovers [S8].

**Known:** the vaquita still exists, still calves, and is genetically
capable of coming back; gillnets are the only major threat [S1][S3][S5][S8].
**Unknown:** the true number — 7–10 is an observation in a murky sea,
not a census; whether the calves reach adulthood; whether protection
holds through political change and totoaba prices; whether any vaquitas
swim undetected outside the monitored area [S5][S9]. Honesty requires
saying it plainly: this story can still end either way.

### The future — what would improve its odds

No single hero saves the vaquita. But a handful of very ordinary,
very possible things, held together, could:

1. **Keep the Zero Tolerance Area truly zero.** Patrols, net removal, and
   the hooked blocks work where they are maintained; where enforcement is
   real, netting falls [S6].
2. **Pay fishers fairly to change.** Vaquita-safe gear exists; it becomes
   real when it earns a living wage, so buyouts, gear programs, and
   markets for legally caught seafood matter as much as patrol boats [S3][S6].
3. **Shrink the price.** Demand reduction in consumer countries and
   customs enforcement along the trafficking route attack the chain at
   its first link [S2].
4. **Keep counting.** The acoustic monitors and annual surveys are the
   species' heartbeat monitor; recovery cannot be managed blind [S5].

None of this is guaranteed, and attention by itself saves nothing. But
every measure above has advanced when enough people were watching — and
quietly stalled when they looked away. The vaquita has already outlived
every year we feared would be its last. Frida's calves are the sea's own
answer: given quiet water, the little cow comes back.

### Timeline of evidence

- **1958** — The species is described by scientists Kenneth Norris and
  William McFarland, from skulls found on a beach. It enters science
  already rare [S1].
- **1975–1977** — Mexico bans totoaba fishing; CITES bans international
  totoaba trade [S2].
- **1997** — First full survey: about **567 vaquitas**. The international
  recovery committee CIRVA has just been convened [S4][S6].
- **2005** — Mexico declares a Vaquita Refuge; gillnetting is prohibited
  there on paper, but enforcement is thin [S6].
- **2015** — With roughly 60 animals left, Mexico announces a two-year
  emergency gillnet ban across the vaquita's habitat, with compensation
  for fishers [S6].
- **2017** — The ban becomes permanent (two fisheries excepted). That
  autumn, the sea-pen rescue plan VaquitaCPR is halted when a captured
  female dies of stress: the species cannot be saved in captivity. The
  only path left is the sea itself [S6][S7].
- **2020** — Mexico establishes the **Zero Tolerance Area** [S3][S6].
- **2022** — The Navy sinks 193 hooked concrete blocks; net-setting drops
  sharply [S6]. *Science* publishes the genome study: recovery is
  possible if gillnet deaths stop [S8].
- **2023–2025** — Three consecutive surveys, no substantial decline;
  2025 finds **7–10 vaquitas and one or two calves** [S5][S9].

*Timeline last verified 2026-08-26. A full statistical abundance estimate
from the 2025 effort is expected during 2026 [S5].*

### A reflective prompt

The vaquita's fate is decided by people it will never see, in places it
will never swim — a banquet table, a customs desk, a fishing cooperative,
a phone screen like this one. Where else does distance make harm easy?
And where might it make care possible?
