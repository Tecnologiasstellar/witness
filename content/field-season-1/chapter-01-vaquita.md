## Premium dossier

### Field note

*Vaquita* means "little cow" in Spanish. The name is affectionate and a
little unserious, which suits an animal that has never asked for
attention. It is the smallest porpoise on Earth, shy of boats, dark-eyed,
surfacing with barely a ripple in a warm, shallow, sediment-green sea
where the Colorado River once emptied into Mexico. The whole species lives
in one corner of the Upper Gulf of California — the smallest range of any
marine mammal [S1]. Most people who have spent their lives on that water
have never seen one. This chapter is about how an animal nobody hunts
became the most endangered marine mammal in the world, and why — against
reasonable expectation — its story is not over.

### The threat chain

No one kills vaquitas on purpose. Each link below is documented; the chain
only works when every link holds, which is also why it can be broken.

1. **A belief creates a price.** The totoaba, a large fish that shares the
   vaquita's sea, has a swim bladder prized in Chinese traditional
   medicine and banqueting culture. Dried, a single bladder can sell for
   thousands of dollars — traffickers call it "aquatic cocaine" [S2].
2. **A price creates a fishery.** Totoaba fishing has been illegal in
   Mexico since 1975, and international trade banned under CITES since
   1977 [S2]. The price survived the ban; so the fishery went underground,
   run increasingly by organized trafficking networks.
3. **The fishery chooses one tool.** Totoaba are caught with gillnets —
   walls of mesh, invisible underwater, sized so that a large animal's
   body wedges in and holds [S3].
4. **The tool cannot tell species apart.** A vaquita that swims into a
   gillnet — set for totoaba, or for shrimp and finfish — cannot back out
   and cannot reach the surface to breathe [S1][S3].
5. **A small population has no cushion.** From roughly 567 animals in
   1997 to fewer than a dozen today, each accidental death now removes a
   meaningful fraction of the species [S4][S5].

The chain has a human hinge: legal fishing communities in San Felipe and
El Golfo de Santa Clara were built on the same nets. A ban without real
alternatives pushes honest fishers toward the illegal economy it is meant
to stop — which is why every serious recovery plan pairs enforcement with
viable vaquita-safe gear and buyout programs [S3][S6].

### Timeline of evidence

- **1958** — The species is formally described by scientists Kenneth
  Norris and William McFarland, from skulls found on a beach. The vaquita
  enters science already rare [S1].
- **1975–1977** — Mexico bans totoaba fishing; CITES bans international
  totoaba trade [S2].
- **1997** — First full survey: about **567 vaquitas**. The international
  recovery committee CIRVA has just been convened [S4][S6].
- **2005** — Mexico declares a Vaquita Refuge in the core of the range.
  Gillnetting is prohibited there — on paper; enforcement is thin [S6].
- **2015** — With an estimated ~60 animals left, Mexico announces a
  two-year emergency gillnet ban across the vaquita's habitat, with
  compensation for fishers [S6].
- **2017** — The gillnet ban is made permanent (with exceptions for two
  fisheries). That autumn, an emergency plan to house the last vaquitas in
  a sea pen — VaquitaCPR — is halted when a captured adult female dies of
  stress; the species, it turns out, cannot be saved in captivity. The
  only path left is the sea itself [S6][S7].
- **2020** — Mexico establishes the **Zero Tolerance Area**: a small
  polygon of the Upper Gulf where the remaining vaquitas cluster and where
  no fishing vessels are permitted at all [S3][S6].
- **2022** — The Mexican Navy sinks 193 concrete blocks topped with metal
  hooks into the Zero Tolerance Area to snag and destroy gillnets.
  Net-setting inside the area drops sharply [S6]. The same year, a
  whole-genome study of 20 vaquitas published in *Science* delivers the
  decade's most important sentence: the vaquita is **not** doomed by
  inbreeding. Its long history of natural rarity left it carrying
  unusually little harmful variation. In simulations, if gillnet deaths
  stop, the species most likely recovers [S8].
- **2023–2025** — Three consecutive annual surveys find **no substantial
  further decline**: 8–12 individuals seen in 2023, 6–8 in 2024 (the
  lowest ever), then **7–10 in 2025 — with one or two newborn calves**.
  One mother, recognizable by a bent dorsal fin and named Frida, has been
  seen with calves in three separate years [S5][S9].

*Timeline last verified 2026-08-26. A full statistical abundance estimate
from the 2025 effort is expected during 2026 [S5].*

### Known / Unknown

**Known.** The vaquita still exists, still reproduces, and is genetically
capable of recovery [S5][S8]. Gillnet entanglement is the only major
threat — there is no evidence that pollution, boat traffic, or food
shortage drives the decline [S1][S3]. Where enforcement is real, as inside
the Zero Tolerance Area since 2022, illegal netting falls [S6].

**Unknown.** The true current population — surveys count sightings in a
murky sea, so 7–10 is an observation, not a census [S5]. Whether the
recent calves survive to adulthood. Whether enforcement and fisher
alternatives hold through political change and totoaba-price pressure.
And whether vaquitas persist undetected outside the small monitored area
[S9]. Honest accounting requires saying so: this story can still end
either way.

### A reflective prompt

The vaquita's recovery depends on people it will never see, in places it
will never swim — a banquet table, a customs desk, a fishing cooperative,
a phone screen like this one. Where else in your life does distance make
harm easy? Where might it make care possible?
