## Premium dossier

### Field note

Every wild red wolf on Earth — every single one — lives in one green
corner of eastern North Carolina, on a peninsula of pocosin swamp and
farm fields about the size of a small country county [S1]. There are
roughly two dozen of them. And every red wolf alive, wild or captive,
descends from just fourteen animals: the founders, caught in the late
1970s when catching them all was the only way left to save them [S2].

The red wolf is smaller than its gray cousin — long-legged, cinnamon
behind the ears — and it lives the way wolves actually live: not as a
horde but as a family, a mother and father and their kids [S2]. This is
the story of a species that has already died out once, came back, nearly
died again, and this spring filled four dens with pups.

### The past — the wolf we removed, then missed

For two centuries, the American South ran a campaign against its
predators — bounties, traps, poison — while the forests themselves were
cleared. The red wolf's range collapsed to the Gulf Coast of Texas and
Louisiana, and as the wolves grew rare, coyotes moved into the emptiness
and began absorbing them, litter by mixed litter [S3]. By the 1970s the
species was dissolving. So the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service did
something desperate: it caught every red wolf it could find and, in
1980, declared the species **extinct in the wild**. Seventeen animals
were captured; fourteen proved to be pure red wolves. Those fourteen
became the ancestors of every red wolf that has lived since [S2][S4].

Seven years later came the redemption: in 1987, four pairs were released
at Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge in North Carolina — the
first time anywhere on Earth that a carnivore declared extinct in the
wild was returned to it. The first wild litter arrived within a year
[S2]. By the late 2000s, around 120 to 130 red wolves ran wild [S2][S3].
Then it unraveled: wolves shot in the confusion of coyote hunting,
packs fractured, releases suspended in 2015 — and by 2020, only about
seven known wolves remained [S5].

### The present — the second second-chance

In January 2021, a federal court ruled the no-release policy unlawful
and ordered the wolves' keepers back to work; a 2023 settlement locked
in eight years of annual release plans [S6]. The recovery is now
visibly, carefully, breathing again. This spring — 2026 — **four wild
litters were born, twelve to sixteen pups surviving their first months**,
one litter on a private landowner's property enrolled in a coexistence
program [S1]. About 280 more red wolves stand ready in breeding centers
across the country [S7].

The program's inventiveness is quietly moving. Biologists sterilize
territorial coyotes and release them as "placeholders" — they hold the
ground without breeding until a wolf family displaces them [S8]. In
April 2025, for the first time ever, a captive-born pup was slipped into
a wild litter, and the wild mother raised it as her own [S7]. The
deadliest remaining enemy is ordinary: a highway. Vehicle strikes on
US-64 are now the leading cause of death — eight wolves since 2020,
including a father whose five pups did not survive his loss [S1][S9].

**Known:** all wild red wolves live in that one five-county recovery
area; the species descends from fourteen founders; the collapse to
about seven has turned back to roughly two dozen plus this spring's
pups [S1][S2][S5]. **Unknown:** the true count — only collared wolves
are "known," so every number is a floor; and the wolf's very origins
are honestly debated (its genome sits close to the coyote's, though a
National Academies review affirmed it as a distinct species worth
saving) [S10]. The debate is real. The animal in the den, raising pups
in the world's last red wolf territory, is not.

### The future — what would improve its odds

1. **Build the underpasses.** A $25 million federal grant, announced
   December 2024 and matched to about $31 million, will put eleven
   wildlife crossings and fencing under US-64 — turning the deadliest
   road into a doorway. Construction is planned for the late 2020s
   [S9][S11].
2. **Keep the pipeline flowing.** The settlement guarantees annual
   releases; 33 captive breeding pairs were matched this season, and
   cross-fostering now moves genes both ways between pens and dens [S7].
3. **Make neighbors into partners.** Prey for the Pack and the Red Wolf
   Coalition pay and support the landowners whose fields the wolves
   share — this spring's private-land litter is what that looks like
   when it works [S1].
4. **Grow beyond one corner.** The official recovery plan calls for
   three wild populations — 740 wolves — before the species is
   considered safe, a fifty-year project whose next two homes are still
   unchosen. And in the coyotes of the Gulf Coast, scientists have found
   "ghost" red wolf genes — a possible reservoir of lost diversity for
   a species built on fourteen ancestors [S12][S13].

As the program's coordinator put it: "We cannot recover this species on
our own... it's the relationships, the trust, the collaboration, that
really determine what is achievable" [S5]. The red wolf has now been
given a second chance twice. This spring, four dens said it was worth
it.

### Timeline of evidence

- **1973** — The red wolf is listed under the new Endangered Species
  Act [S2].
- **1980** — Declared extinct in the wild; fourteen of seventeen
  captured wolves become the founders of every red wolf since [S2][S4].
- **1987** — Four pairs released at Alligator River, North Carolina —
  the world's first carnivore reintroduction; first wild litter in
  1988 [S2].
- **Late 2000s** — The wild population peaks around 120–130 [S2][S3].
- **2015–2020** — Releases suspended; the population collapses to about
  seven known wolves [S5].
- **2021–2023** — A federal court orders releases resumed; a settlement
  locks in eight years of annual release plans [S6].
- **2023** — The revised recovery plan sets the long goal: three wild
  populations, 740 wolves, roughly fifty years [S12].
- **2024** — December: $25 million granted for eleven US-64 wildlife
  crossings [S9].
- **2025** — April: the first-ever red wolf cross-foster — a captive
  pup raised by a wild mother [S7].
- **2026** — Four wild litters; twelve to sixteen pups; roughly two
  dozen known adults [S1].

*Timeline last verified 2026-08-26.*

### A reflective prompt

The red wolf exists because people admitted a mistake and then spent
fifty years repairing it — and the repair is still not finished. What
mistake would you be willing to spend that long making right?
